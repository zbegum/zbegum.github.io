The FATİH program (launched 2010, Ministry of National Education) is Turkey's national education-technology initiative. Its design target group is the country's K–12 student population — approximately 17 million children and one million teachers and administrators. Its stated objective is the reduction of educational inequality through technology integration. The deployed solution has three components: tablets for students and teachers, interactive whiteboards in classrooms, and a national content network (EBA, on a state-managed VPN). The program was personally championed by the prime minister at launch and branded as Asrın Projesi — "the project of the century" — before any educational outcome existed to assess.

My position relative to the program is not external. I was one of the students the promise was addressed to: a child in the Turkish state-education system while the rollout was being announced, celebrated, and unevenly delivered. As a student, the program assumed that access to a device would translate into access to better learning. Through my brother's schooling, I saw another version of the same assumption. His school received FATİH tablets, but they were not meaningfully applied in his education; across his school career, the devices remained closer to a distribution event than an everyday learning tool. They existed more as objects that had arrived than as tools that changed classroom practice. At home, the program was also visible through my parents, both teachers. For teachers, the program assumed that educators could absorb new hardware, platforms, and policy language into their work without the corresponding infrastructure, training, repair support, or pedagogical redesign. So this critique comes from several points inside the target group — student, sibling, and teacher household — and from the gap between the program's promise and what it actually made possible.

Exhibit B · looking back
003 / the critique

what the tablet could not carry.

— reading the program by what it actually built and measured.

a device is not a pedagogy.

the program treated the gap between hardware and learning as something the hardware would, on its own, eventually close. the gap is what this critique is about.

"You cannot solve non-technological problems with technology."

— Ziya Selçuk, then Minister of National Education, addressing an education union — about the program his own ministry was administering.

From an HCI perspective, this statement crystallizes the central technosolutionist mistake: education — a relational, infrastructural, deeply political practice — was treated as if a deployed artifact (a tablet, a board, a content network) could replace it. That a former Minister of National Education names this limit publicly is itself notable. It places Selçuk among the program's critics rather than its defenders, and signals how widely the technosolutionist diagnosis is recognized — including by people who have held responsibility for state-tech programs of this kind.

hardware as the deliverable.

From a user-centered design standpoint, the FATİH program was mostly evaluated through distribution metrics: how many tablets were purchased, how many boards were installed, and how much of the budget was spent. These numbers make the program look measurable and concrete, but they do not show whether the technology actually supported teaching and learning. This is a key HCI problem. A sociotechnical system is not successful simply because the artifact exists. It is successful only when the artifact becomes part of a usable and meaningful workflow. The 2012 pilot had already identified problems such as activation failures, missing content, insufficient teacher training, and weak supply chains. Later audit reports repeated similar concerns at a larger scale. Yet the public language around the program continued to focus on procurement and distribution rather than classroom use. By 2024, the ministry reported 2,406,716 tablets distributed, although the original promise was a “tablet for every student.” Similarly, around 432,288 interactive whiteboards were purchased, but only about 197,520 were installed. By 2022, four out of every ten classrooms still did not have a board. Spending exceeded 15 billion lira, with a 68.7% realization rate. These figures mainly describe logistical output. They do not answer whether teachers and students could actually access, use, and benefit from the system in everyday educational practice.

technology as a power symbol.

The issue is not simply that technology was introduced into schools, but how it was introduced and what kind of power relations it produced. In this case tablets are presented as "the best solution" that will modernize education assuming they are "neutral tools". This framing hides the political and institutional decisions behind the technology: who selects it, who funds it, who benefits from it, and who is expected to adapt to it. The structural pattern was top-down rather than participatory. Procurement decisions, ministerial offices, and vendor leadership were positioned upstream of the school, while teachers and students were positioned downstream. This means that decision-making power remained with institutions and technical actors, while the everyday work of making the technology usable happened inside classrooms. This connects to our class discussion on robots and technological dominance. Robots and digital systems are often imagined as efficient, objective, and superior to human practices, but this can make the human labor around them invisible. Similarly, educational technology can appear as progress, while teachers and students are expected to reorganize their routines around it. In this sense, the device becomes more than a learning tool. Therefore, when technology is imposed from above, it risks becoming a governance tool rather than a participatory educational tool.

the COVID stress test.

The 2020 pandemic accidentally became the first large-scale stress test of what the program had actually achieved. The ministry’s own statistics showed that many students could not access online instruction at all. Even among those who could, many joined classes through personal mobile phones rather than FATİH-issued tablets. From an HCI perspective, this reveals a key design and evaluation problem: the program treated the existence of devices as if it automatically meant functional access for students. However, having devices in the system is not the same as making them available, usable, maintained, and connected for the people who need them. Read together with the audit findings and the ministerial admission discussed above, the conclusion is clear. The problems were not simply unexpected technical failures. They were structural issues that had already been recognized by institutions with the authority to change the program. Yet the program continued in the same top-down form, without fundamentally addressing these weaknesses.

Diagnosis: known. Response: none.

Exhibit C · approaching it again
004 / the reframe

what was not done.

— four things a real education investment would have required.

A values-sensitive version of the program would have inverted its order of operations. The four commitments below correspond to specific HCI subdisciplines the program declined.

01

contextual inquiry first.

Ethnographic fieldwork in classrooms across the design target group's full range — rural, urban, well-resourced, under-resourced — before any procurement decision, to determine whether hardware is the appropriate intervention at all.

What the program did instead: the 2012 pilot was a deployment test, not design research.

— contextual inquiry / ethnographic HCI
02

participatory design with binding authority.

Teachers, students, parents, IT coordinators, and repair technicians at the design table with decisional power — not advisory consultation. User-centered design studies users; participatory design involves them as designers.

What the program did instead: consulted teachers as users-to-be-trained; excluded students, parents, and local technical staff.

— participatory design / co-design
03

infrastructure as the deliverable.

Reliable broadband, repair capacity, electricity, content management, and accessible technical support as phase one. A device shipped into infrastructure that does not yet exist is an artifact placed onto a hope, not a sociotechnical system.

What the program did instead: shipped hardware first; billed schools for VPN they could not pay.

— infrastructure studies / sustainable HCI
04

repair, refusal, reversibility.

Right-to-repair for local technicians, opt-out for schools where the tool is not working, contracts permitting non-renewal on poor metrics, public outcome reporting tied to the program's original promise.

What the program did instead: IMEI-locked devices; ~234,000 boards lost between purchase and installation; metrics tracked publicly were units shipped.

— repair & maintenance HCI / feminist HCI

There is much more to discuss about the FATİH project, and many parts of it could have been fundamentally different. To reframe this from an HCI perspective one could argue that the program should not have started with procurement, distribution targets, or hardware-first promises. It should have started with classrooms, users, infrastructure, maintenance, and participation. A more responsible design process would have asked whether tablets and smartboards were the right intervention, involved teachers and students in real decision-making, treated infrastructure and repair as core deliverables, and allowed schools to adapt, refuse, or reverse technologies that did not work in practice. In this sense, the alternative was not simply “better technology,” but a more democratic and situated sociotechnical design process.

006 — reframe
Coda · a small thought to end on

FATIH is technosolutionism at national scale. Its record clarifies the pattern: treating education as a logistics problem produced a procurement system, not a learning system. Treating teachers, students, and parents as recipients rather than co-designers reproduced existing asymmetries instead of addressing them. Treating units shipped as the metric meant the program continued meeting the metrics it set for itself, while failing the metrics it had originally named. After fourteen years and over fifteen billion lira, the case is not closed because the program has ended it has not but because what it does has become legible.
